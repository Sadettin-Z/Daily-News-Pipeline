### 1. French high school protests escalate as teenager loses hand to police grenade ahead of nationwide marches

France is facing its biggest youth protest movement in decades, which began on September 21 at Saint-Exupéry high school in a Paris suburb when pupils backed teachers striking over staff shortages and spread nationwide to Lille, Marseille, Montpellier, Nantes, Toulouse, Nice and Bordeaux. Students are protesting overcrowded classes, crumbling buildings, teacher shortages, unsanitary toilets and the Parcoursup admissions platform; a 2010 birth spike has pushed 250,000 more pupils into secondary schools that have fewer teachers than in 2008, and 70% of public classrooms exceed 30 pupils. Nearly 590 of the country's 3,700 high schools were still disrupted on Monday, October 5, more than 500 were partly or fully closed, and Education Minister Edouard Geffray said 24 schools had been burned or ransacked and 190 students injured, blaming accidents and troublemakers; the government says at least 215 teenagers, 715 police officers and 85 teaching staff have been hurt, and the justice ministry reports 5,060 arrests, 87% of them minors, with more than 1,800 prosecuted and 790 cases dismissed. Damage in the Paris region is estimated at 7 million euros. The most serious injuries occurred during police interventions: around 10 am on Monday outside Auguste-Béhal high school in Lens, a 15-year-old pupil had his hand torn off by a grenade, possibly a GM2L device that cost a protester her thumb in Rouen in 2023; the prefecture said 200 to 250 pupils had pelted officers with stones and fireworks, a witness said riot police threw the grenade on a second charge, the boy was taken to a clinic in Lille and is not in mortal danger, and the IGPN police inspectorate opened an investigation. Another student in Tours lost an eye, a boy's jaw and teeth were shattered, a girl was hit in the head by a tear gas canister and a policeman was filmed using a now-illegal headlock on a girl in Alençon; ten brutality investigations are under way and human rights groups accuse riot police of disproportionate force against children. Student leader Manès Nadel said the Education Ministry was totally overwhelmed; Geffray met student leaders Friday without agreement, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced measures on improved school buildings, better planned school days and a more transparent Parcoursup plus an accelerated 'who destroys pays' bill, which the USL student union dismissed as nothing concrete, and a planned 2027 fee for technological high schools was dropped. Researchers from a three-year University of Tours study of nearly 200 interviews and 7,000 surveys argue the movement expresses long-ignored problems with learning conditions, with one in two students reluctant to use school toilets. Pupils chant against Marine Le Pen, who leads polls for the 2027 presidential election, while the government blames Jean-Luc Mélenchon's LFI, whose leader called it the strongest youth movement since 1968, and Geffray alleged far-left infiltration. Mass marches by students, teachers' union SNES-FSU, trade unionists and parents were set for Tuesday, October 6. The unrest coincides with fiscal turmoil: France's deficit reached 5.8% of GDP, debt stands at 3.596 trillion euros or 119% of GDP, the 10-year yield briefly topped 5% last week, borrowing costs have climbed to 4.5% with annual interest payments expected to reach 65 billion euros, the Franco-German spread widened to 141 to 150 basis points in the largest weekly move since 1990, and the euro fell to about 1.116 dollars, with analysts at ING and Deutsche Bank questioning whether Lecornu's 54 billion euro austerity package for a 2027 budget targeting a deficit near 5% can calm markets as regrouped Yellow Vests demand more education funding, frozen energy prices and taxes on large companies.

### 2. Saudi-backed Yemeni forces launch offensive to retake Red Sea coast and Bab al-Mandab from Houthis

A Saudi-led coalition and forces loyal to Yemen's internationally recognised government launched a major offensive on Monday, named Operation Dawn of Yemen, to retake the Red Sea coast and the Bab al-Mandab Strait from the Iran-backed Houthis, reviving memories of Saudi Arabia's earlier war in Yemen that became a quagmire. Presidential Leadership Council head Rashad al-Alimi announced the operation on Sunday with the aim of recapturing all Houthi-held territory, including Sanaa, held since 2014. Coalition spokesman Maj Gen Turki al-Maliki said 100 fighter jets were supporting the push, that the coalition had secured air protection of the strait, destroyed 324 high-value targets and besieged hundreds of fighters in Dhubab district including its airfield, with some fleeing by boat; strikes also hit command centres, camps and depots in Sanaa and Saada, a ballistic missile launch platform in Sanaa and a Saada facility holding 20 missiles, while naval strikes on Hodeidah hit depots for explosive boats and naval mines. Government forces said they took Dhubab, Bab, Hadeid and the Mokha port and the al-Manzara, al-Ashari and Dhiban mountains in Haifan south of Taiz; government sources told Reuters and AFP forces had only reached Mokha's outskirts, and the Houthis denied losing Mokha, Dhubab, Bab al-Mandab or the Kahboub mountains, calling the claims fabricated heroics. The UAE-backed Giants Brigades, linked to the dissolved Southern Transitional Council, opened a ground front on the eastern flank, a reversal after Saudi jets bombed UAE arms shipments to STC forces in January. The Houthis had swept the Red Sea coast in September after ending a 2022 ceasefire, seized Turbah, cut the Taiz-Aden supply route at al-Safiya and surrounded Taiz; fighting in Taiz and Lahj killed more than 70 people in a day, 34 government troops and 39 Houthi fighters. The Houthis retaliated with missiles and drones at King Khalid airport in Riyadh, Abha airport, Aramco refineries in Riyadh and Rabigh and the Khurais facility, with BBC Verify imagery showing smoke at the Riyadh refinery and Khurais; they warned airlines Saudi airspace outside Mecca and Medina was a theatre of operations. Saudi Arabia's Civil Aviation Authority confirmed only that King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz airport in Jazan and Najran airport were hit on Monday evening, causing three minor injuries and limited damage; the GCC's Jassim al-Budaiwi called it a dangerous escalation and Kuwait condemned the attacks. Houthi media reported a schoolgirl and a woman killed in Sanaa. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Saudi Arabia and Yemen's government have a right to defend themselves, citing a US-Saudi defence agreement; WELT's Daniel-Dylan Böhmer said the US is supporting the Saudis with aerial reconnaissance and satellite material, and the UK Ministry of Defence confirmed British jets are providing refuelling. Turkey, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia, meeting in Riyadh, agreed to activate collective deterrence measures under the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement signed in August and rapidly deploy forces to the kingdom; former UN adviser Fernando Carvajal said the practical support remains unclear and that Mokha, surrounded by Houthis on three sides, is the vital test. UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres condemned Houthi cross-border attacks. Since fighting resumed, the WHO counts at least 959 killed and over 4,200 wounded, with 5,000 injured and 184,000 to 197,000 displaced by other counts, and its regional emergency director Annette Heinzelmann warned the health system is near breaking point. Saudi Arabia had been using the Red Sea route for oil exports as an alternative to the Strait of Hormuz, closed by the US-Israel war with Iran, and the Houthi seizure of the strait last month pushed up global oil prices.

### 3. Francis Halzen wins 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for IceCube neutrino observatory

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced in Stockholm on Tuesday, October 6, that the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics goes to Francis Halzen, 82, a Belgian-born astrophysicist and US citizen born in Tienen and based at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. Secretary general Ellen Moons said the prize is about ghostly messengers from space; committee chair Mark Pearce said Halzen's tenacity and scientific vision paved the way for a new kind of astronomy, and member Eva Olsson explained that nearly massless, chargeless neutrinos pass through matter unchanged and carry information about distant galaxies, exploding stars and regions hidden behind dust clouds and black holes. Halzen first proposed the detector in 1988; its predecessor AMANDA, completed in 2000, failed to catch the targeted neutrinos, while IceCube, a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice at the South Pole fitted with 5,160 light sensors on 86 cables, reached full size in 2011, reported the first evidence of cosmic neutrinos in 2013 and confirmed it a couple of years later; an expansion to eight cubic kilometres is planned. Speaking by phone from Italy, Halzen called the award a great surprise, saying very few thought it would work, including himself, and hoped it reflected on the courageous people who joined the project early. Physics Today's Richard Fitzgerald noted the rarity of a prize to a single individual; Louis Barson of the Institute of Physics and Oxford's Subir Sarkar said IceCube opened a new window on the universe. Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever noted Halzen is the 11th Belgian laureate and the second in physics. The prize is worth 12 million Swedish kronor, about 1.2 million dollars, and will be presented by King Carl XVI Gustaf in Stockholm on December 10, the anniversary of Alfred Nobel's death. Monday's medicine prize went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for optogenetics, a light-based method to switch neural circuits on and off that has given partial sight to people blinded by retinitis pigmentosa in clinical trials; chemistry follows Wednesday, literature Thursday, peace Friday in Oslo and economics on October 12. Last year's physics prize went to John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis for quantum mechanics experiments.

### 4. Former German spy chief August Hanning arrested on espionage and treason charges

German federal prosecutors said August Hanning, 80, president of the Federal Intelligence Service (BND) from December 1998 to November 2005, was arrested on Tuesday, October 6, on suspicion of treasonous espionage, spying out state secrets, attempted treason and espionage; reports place the arrest in Bad Tölz-Wolfratshausen or at his home in Nordwalde near Münster. His former chief of staff, identified as Manfred D., who went on to serve as chief of staff to several subsequent BND presidents from March 2012 until March 2026, was arrested the same day in Steinfurt or Bavaria and charged with aiding and abetting attempted treason and treasonous espionage. Prosecutors allege the two agreed in spring 2010 that Manfred D. would continuously pass BND intelligence to Hanning in exchange for remuneration, supplying roughly 2,000 documents over the years, many classified and intended solely for internal executive intelligence briefings. Hanning allegedly used the material in the private consulting business he built after leaving the BND, on one occasion drafted an analysis for an officer of a foreign intelligence service, and for several years passed political information to a representative of another foreign service; prosecutors have not named the countries and say it is unclear whether the material reached its intended recipient. Premises linked to both men were searched in several locations including Berlin, with digital material seized; Manfred D. was to appear before judges at the federal court of justice in Karlsruhe on Tuesday and Hanning on Wednesday. The case grew out of a separate investigation into Hamburg steakhouse heiress Christina Block, accused of orchestrating the kidnapping of her two youngest children from her ex-husband in Denmark in January 2024; prosecutors said in autumn 2025 that Hanning and a retired Hamburg police official are suspected of accepting a 2022 contract from Block, which Hanning denies, and a search of his residence turned up a storage device with recent BND documents. Hanning, considered well connected in Russia and China, served as top security official under interior minister Wolfgang Schäuble after 2005. Greens lawmaker Konstantin von Notz called the allegations deeply troubling and urged a ban on former top-clearance officials doing private consulting; the arrest shook the German political world. In 2023 BND officer Carsten L. was charged with passing secrets on Ukraine and Wagner to Russia for roughly 400,000 euros.

### 5. Parti Québécois wins Quebec election with minority government, reviving independence push

The separatist Parti Québécois under Paul St-Pierre Plamondon won Quebec's provincial election on Monday, October 5, returning to power for the first time since 2014 after voters went to the polls with the party leading. Projections and incomplete results gave the PQ 59 of 127 seats in the National Assembly, short of the 64 needed for a majority, on about 28% of the popular vote, ahead of the Parti Libéral du Québec with 40, the Parti Conservateur du Québec with 19 and Québec Solidaire with nine; the governing Coalition Avenir Québec, in power since 2018 under François Legault and holding 79 seats, was shut out entirely, and his successor Christine Fréchette lost her own seat in Trois-Rivières. St-Pierre Plamondon, who took the party from three MPs after 2022 to nearly 60, told supporters chanting 'Québec, a country!' that the PQ had rekindled hope, called a referendum an act of democracy that is up to no one but Quebecers to determine, framed sovereignty as a question of survival for the French-speaking population and said he would need help from pro-independence lawmakers in other parties; he has pledged to wait until Donald Trump leaves office, implying 2029 or 2030, and promised a French-speaking, prosperous and exemplary democratic country. Independence polls at about 30% after referendum defeats in 1980 and 1995, and McGill's Daniel Béland said the vote was about ousting the CAQ rather than sovereignty; Liberal leader Charles Milliard said his party would not collaborate on a referendum, though Québec Solidaire could hold the balance of power. St-Pierre Plamondon also vowed to fight Ottawa on immigration and to abolish the monarchy in Quebec by defunding the lieutenant-governor's office, a move facing legal challenges. Prime Minister Mark Carney congratulated him and pledged cooperation on US tariffs, jobs and housing, but faces a possible second unity crisis when Alberta votes on October 19 on whether to hold its own separation referendum. Oxford Economics projects a C$2bn hit to Quebec from US tariffs imposed in August, and Trump's tariffs and 51st-state rhetoric have stirred Canadian nationalism that complicates the separatist case. Quebec, about 80% French-speaking with 9.1 million people, rejected sovereignty narrowly in 1995.

### 6. US pulls B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford after intelligence of Iran-linked threat

The United States withdrew about a dozen, or at least ten, B-1 long-range bombers from RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire to their US home stations over the weekend with unusual speed, a move a Pentagon official confirmed on October 4 and flight-tracking sites recorded the same day. President Donald Trump said on Monday the aircraft 'didn't fly out by accident' but left because of 'some threats', that Washington knows who made them and that they would be linked to Iran, adding Iran would pay a high price if it had brought combat drones to Britain while declining to confirm details. The Wall Street Journal, citing US and European officials, reported urgent concern about a complex attack ordered by Iran's Revolutionary Guards as part of a multi-layered plot to hit the aircraft and kill personnel including US pilots, Axios reported intelligence of a possible Iranian drone attack, and the New York Times said US commanders received new, specific threat information within 24 hours; sources told the BBC the concern may have involved sabotage or strikes if hostilities with Iran resumed, citing Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb, which smuggled drones into Russia on lorries. The base has been under counter-terror investigation since September 27, when police were alerted to three suspicious vehicles: five London-based British men aged 23 to 25 were arrested on explosives and then terror-preparation offences and bailed, drawing criticism from Trump and the Pentagon, and a sixth, a 25-year-old UK-Iranian dual national, was also arrested and bailed; whether they are connected to the drone plot is unclear. Prime Minister Andy Burnham cited strong indications of Iranian involvement, British sources said the decision was military and operational rather than political, and Tehran firmly denies it, having said in July that any base used against it is a legitimate target. Vice-President JD Vance called the move an abundance of caution, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio spoke of a foreign actor, said rotation of assets is not unusual, drew no direct link to the plot and praised British cooperation. The US had moved extra bombers to Britain in March, and B-1s and B-52s carrying bunker-busting bombs and air-to-ground missiles had flown from Fairford to the Middle East since March 1 after former prime minister Keir Starmer approved defensive strikes on Iranian missile sites following the US-Israeli attacks on Iran that began February 28. RUSI analyst Christoph Bergs said the redeployment reduces US capacity to surge sorties against Iran and removes its basing option outside continental Europe, with Diego Garcia or Gulf bases as alternatives. The Guardian's Dan Sabbagh discussed the withdrawal in a podcast.

### 7. Flávio Bolsonaro's shock first-round win puts him on course for Brazil's presidency

Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, eldest son of imprisoned former president Jair Bolsonaro, won the first round of Brazil's presidential election on Sunday with 56.1 million votes (47.03%) against 53.8 million (45.16%) for the 80-year-old incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, ahead of a runoff on October 25; since 1985 no first-round runner-up has won the second round, and political scientist Miguel Lago said he does not see Flávio losing now, though analyst Mello Franco cautioned the contest is not over. Flávio won more votes than his father did in 2018 and 2022. Donald Trump called it a very big victory, Argentina's Javier Milei hailed a historic win over 'the communist Lula', and Benjamin Netanyahu looked forward to restoring friendship between Israel and Brazil; as favourite, Flávio would give Trump a critical new ally in Latin America, particularly on security, in a major boost for the right that leaves Lula three weeks to halt the region's rightward shift. The far right swept congress: Jair Bolsonaro, 71, under house arrest serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup, saw his wife Michelle, 44, named the most powerful woman in Brazilian politics by a July poll, and son Carlos, 43, a Rio councillor indicted for leading a so-called hate cabinet, elected senators, while son Jair Renan, 28, and brother Renato, 62, who had lost seven straight elections, won lower-house seats; Eduardo, 42, sentenced in absentia to four years for coercion over lobbying the Trump administration, remains in US exile and reportedly hopes to become foreign minister. ### A history of presidents naming things after themselves (It's short. They rarely do)

Lindsay Chervinsky, executive director of the George Washington Presidential Library, said the question arose with the Coinage Act of 1792, when the House of Representatives decided the president should not appear on currency because of the monarchical association; Washington "didn't want a lot of fuss," though the federal city was named for him while in office. Dams named for Teddy Roosevelt, Calvin Coolidge and Woodrow Wilson were pushed by supporters rather than the presidents. The Boulder Dam was renamed the Hoover Dam in 1930 at the suggestion of Interior Secretary Ray Lyman Wilbur, since Hoover had negotiated the Colorado River Compact among seven states; Franklin D. Roosevelt's Interior Secretary Harold Ickes reverted the name in 1933, and Roosevelt called it "Boulder Dam" in his 1935 dedication, before Congress unanimously restored the Hoover name in 1947. Roosevelt did initiate his own presidential library. Trump has said he plans a presidential library in Miami that "most likely" will be a hotel with a 747 Air Force One in the lobby, and a rendering on his social media shows his name prominently on the facade.

Financial Times'a göre Putin 2021'de Soçi'yi 24 kez ziyaret ederken, Ukrayna insansız hava araçlarının Karadeniz kıyısına daha sık ulaştığı 2024 ve 2025'te yalnızca iki kez gitti. Yıkım 2024'te başladı; Eylül 2025'te alanda büyük bir kazı alanı oluştu, 2026 sonbaharındaki uydu görüntülerinde ise eskisinden çok daha geniş alana yayılan yeni binaların çatıları görüldü. Federal Koruma Servisi (FSO) bu yıl konut çevresinde üç kilometrekareden büyük bir güvenlik bölgesi oluşturmaya karar verdi. 2014 Kış Olimpiyatları için yapılan Boçarov Ruçey-2 binası yerinde duruyor; Putin 2024'te UAEA Başkanı Rafael Grossi'yi burada ağırladı. Mahkeme kararlarında FSO'nun 2023-2027 dönemini kapsayan devlet savunma tedarik sözleşmesine atıf yapılıyor; ana yüklenicinin ilhak edilen Kırım'da kayıtlı TSI Group olduğu belirlendi. Sivastopol'daki Voenmorproekt-30 enstitüsü 2025 başında başlayan inşaatta takvimin gerisinde kaldı, Orenburg merkezli bir şirketin ana bina duvar işlerinde usulsüzlük tespit edildi. Ocak 2025'ten itibaren yayımlanan ilanlar, giriş kartı ve pasaport kontrolü gerektiren "stratejik" tesiste elden ödeme, havuzlu yurt ve günde üç öğün yemek vadetti; başvurular önce sabıkasız Rus vatandaşlarıyla sınırlıyken sonra Belarus, Kazakistan ve Kırgızistan vatandaşlarına açıldı. TSI'ya bağlı Transstroiinvest daha önce Kırım'ın Oliva yerleşiminde Putin için bir konut inşa etmiş, 2022-2025'te işgal altındaki Kırım'daki Artek Uluslararası Çocuk Merkezi için yaklaşık 330 milyon dolarlık 54 sözleşme almıştı ve Mariupol'deki Pobeda sinemasını restore ediyor. TSI'nın nihai sahibi olduğu düşünülen 47 yaşındaki Aleksey Fomenko, Putin'e yakın iş insanı Arkadi Rotenberg ve Kremlin Sözcüsü Dmitri Peskov sorulara yanıt vermedi.

### Fon krizi için TBMM'de araştırma komisyonu tartışması: Partiler ne diyor?

Fon işlemlerinde usulsüzlük ve bazı isimlerin önceden bilgi alarak sistemden çıktığı iddialarıyla gündeme gelen kriz, TBMM'nin yeni yasama yılının ilk tartışma başlıklarından biri olacak. Yeni Parti, DEM Parti, CHP ve İYİ Parti başta olmak üzere muhalefet, AKP Genel Başkan Yardımcılığı'ndan istifa eden Fatma Betül Sayan Kaya dahil fondan yüksek kazanç elde ettiği öne sürülen isimler için Meclis araştırması önergesi vermeye hazırlanıyor. İktidar ise krizin başında masada olan araştırma komisyonu seçeneğinden, muhalefetin süreci aleyhine kullanabileceği ve konunun uzun süre gündemde kalmasının ekonomiyi olumsuz etkileyebileceği gerekçesiyle vazgeçti; AKP kaynakları konunun yargıya intikal ettiğini ve Devlet Denetleme Kurulu'nun inceleme başlattığını belirtiyor. Bunun yerine İçtüzük'ün 59. maddesi uyarınca Fon Koordinasyon Kurulu'nun başındaki Cumhurbaşkanı Yardımcısı Cevdet Yılmaz'ın Genel Kurul'u bilgilendirmesi ve ardından parti gruplarının söz alması planlanıyor. ### Fatma Betül Sayan Kaya ve eşinden fon kazançları için ödeme başvurusu

Fon iddiaları sonrası AKP'den istifa eden, 2016-2018'de Aile ve Sosyal Politikalar Bakanı olan Fatma Betül Sayan Kaya ve eşi İlyas Kaya'nın fon kazançlarını gönüllülük kapsamında ödemesine izin verildi; yedi farklı bankadaki paranın TMSF hesabına transferine izin veren Cumhuriyet Başsavcılığı yazısı basında yer aldı. 26 Eylül'de Sayan Kaya'nın Özata Denizcilik'ten aldığı 63 milyon TL'lik hisseyi kriz patlak vermeden kısa süre önce 1,3 milyar TL'ye sattığı, İlyas Kaya'nın ise yaklaşık 99,7 milyon liralık yatırım karşılığında 826 milyon lira çektiği iddia edilmişti. İstanbul Cumhuriyet Başsavcılığı soruşturmalarında tutuklu sayısı 85'e çıktı. Kriz 16 Eylül'de bazı yatırım fonlarının temerrüte düşmesiyle derinleşmiş; borsadaki sert düşüşlerin ardından SPK 17 Eylül'de Tera, Pusula, Hedef, Atlas, A1, Pardus ve Bulls Portföy'ün 131 fonunu tasfiye etme kararı almış ve bu şirketlerin TEFAS'taki tüm fonlarını işleme kapatmıştı. Soruşturmaya sonradan Katılımevim Tasarruf Finansman AŞ (KTLEV) payı da dahil edildi ve bağlantılı yöneticilerin para ve malvarlığı hareketleri donduruldu. SPK, düşük dolaşımlı hisselerdeki ekonomik gerçeklerle açıklanamayan fiyat hareketlerinin uzun süredir izlendiğini belirtti; tasfiyede Tera Portföy'ü İş Bankası, diğer fonları Ziraat Bankası yönetecek. Kurul eylül sonunda, tasfiye kararından önce payını satarak yüksek kazanç elde edenler için her fon adına "isteğe bağlı iade hesapları" açıldığını duyurmuştu.
