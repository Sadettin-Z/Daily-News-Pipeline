### 1. France's student protest movement broadens as government suspends stun grenades

A high-school protest movement that began in mid-September over missing teachers, overcrowded classrooms and crumbling buildings has spread to hundreds of schools across France, with parents, teachers and unions joining marches; the Interior Ministry counted 256,000 demonstrators on Tuesday while unions claimed 450,000, and a second day of mass protests in a week was set for Thursday. More than 6,000 people, mostly minors, have been arrested. After a 15-year-old lost a hand to an EF-SON stun grenade in Lens on October 5, Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez suspended the grenades' use at student protests, and the police inspectorate has opened 18 investigations into alleged misconduct. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu denied police were ordered to confront students and promised a response plan, while accusing La France insoumise of escalating the unrest. Polls find 72% of French people consider the students' demands legitimate.

### 2. Russia denies plague outbreak after Siberian lab worker's death as WHO seeks answers

Darya Shipilova, a 28-year-old employee of the Anti-Plague Institute in Irkutsk, Siberia, died of what Russia calls pneumonia of unknown origin, prompting quarantines of about 200 contacts and fears of a pneumonic plague outbreak. Russia's health watchdog Rospotrebnadzor says there is no epidemic risk, that nearly 5,000 laboratory tests found no dangerous pathogens and that she had been vaccinated against plague, and the Kremlin dismissed reports of a second case as false information. WHO chief Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said Russia's information was insufficient for a full risk assessment; CNN reports the WHO changed its rating from low to undetermined, while BBC Türkçe cites a WHO assessment of moderate-to-low risk for Irkutsk and low for Russia as a whole. The United States formally requested more details, and President Trump said he does not believe a bioweapon was involved. Experts say any wider spread is unlikely because plague is treatable with common antibiotics.
